\section*{JOB MARKET PAPER}
``Reassessed \& Repossessed: Which Homeowners Bear the Burden of a Reappraisal Shock'', Sole-Authored
\begin{list}{}{\setlength{\leftmargin}{0.5in}\setlength{\rightmargin}{0.5in}\setlength{\topsep}{4pt}}
\item[] \textbf{Abstract:} Most U.S.\ homes are reassessed on multiyear cycles, so appreciation goes untaxed between reappraisals and arrives as a discrete increase in the tax bill in the assessment year. This paper asks who bears the burden of that increase. Mecklenburg County, North Carolina, held assessed values fixed from 2003 to 2011, while its cheapest homes appreciated faster than the rest of the county. Linking administrative tax bills, deed records and a consumer address history, I compare Mecklenburg with counties that reassess every year. After the 2011 reappraisal, quarterly foreclosure filings on the cheapest quartile of homes rise 0.55 percentage points, about one and a half times their pre-period rate, and the effect declines steadily with home value to 0.07 points in the most expensive quartile. At the observed completion rate, 423 low-value households lose a home. The average foreclosed household moves to a neighborhood 9 log points cheaper and becomes a renter. Long reappraisal cycles can induce tax shocks that place the cost of catching up on the owners least able to absorb it.
\end{list}
